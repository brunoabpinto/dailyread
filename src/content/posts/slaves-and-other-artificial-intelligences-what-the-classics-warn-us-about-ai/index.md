The essay begins in Homer's *Iliad*, where the god Hephaestus is attended by golden mechanical handmaids. The author treats these as an early imagining of artificial intelligence, then points out that the ancient world's real version of "intelligence on demand" was enslaved people. She cites Roman examples from Seneca's letters. One wealthy man had enslaved boys named after the letters of the alphabet to stand in for his son's learning. Another had enslaved men memorize Greek poets so he could recite them at dinner and take the credit.

From there the piece argues that effortless intelligence has always depended on someone else's unseen work. She sets Seneca's implicit warning against modern claims that intelligence will soon be cheap or free. Myth supplies further frames. Pandora is an artificial being whose gifts brought ruin, and Prometheus's stolen fire promises productivity without toil. The essay suggests the useful question is less what language models can do than what we are willing to do with them.

The author also looks at how AI products are presented. Their human names, personas and borrowed literary or scientific references make them feel familiar and trustworthy. She turns to Icarus, to Ovid's account of the watchers and to later reenactments. In one, a performer playing the flyer really died on stage. She also describes a ritual in which a condemned man fitted with wings was sacrificed to purify a community. These stories show how societies accept that someone else will absorb the cost of risky ambition.

The conclusion draws the parallel to the present. The modern counterparts of the alphabet-boys are the data annotators and content moderators who train and clean up AI systems, often in poorly paid roles in countries such as Kenya and Uganda. They are joined by remote workers who quietly support systems that appear to be automated. The classics, she argues, make us look at the human infrastructure behind machine intelligence. Keeping up the illusion of a labor-free technology risks treating it as something to be worshipped.
