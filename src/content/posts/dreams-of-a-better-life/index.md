The essay opens with a scene from *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest*. Denied a broadcast of the World Series, McMurphy narrates the game aloud to the ward's patients while they cheer at a blank television. Beckerman reads this as a dissident's trick, which Joseph Brodsky called "the science of ignoring reality": you behave as though the world you want already exists.

He traces this "as if" posture through several settings. Polish dissidents of the early 1980s lived as if they were free, and Thoreau's stay at Walden and his refusal to pay a poll tax can be read as performances of an alternative system of values. Beckerman also questions the stance. It can slide into self-regard, and Thoreau, like Solzhenitsyn, underplayed how hard it is for ordinary people to move from compromise to principle.

Next come the Soviet dissidents, who were mostly legalists, not revolutionaries. The mathematician Alexander Volpin urged them to take the state's own laws at their word. After the 1965 arrests of the writers Sinyavsky and Daniel, he called for a "glasnost meeting" in Pushkin Square, demanding open trials as the constitution promised. The samizdat *Chronicle of Current Events* printed a human-rights guarantee on its cover. Beckerman links this to Ernst Bloch's "anticipatory consciousness" and his idea of the "not-yet," a future that people act toward before it exists.

The central case is Poland in 1980. A shipyard strike grew into Solidarity, a movement of roughly nine million people that built a "parallel polis" alongside the official state. It was outlawed under martial law, survived underground, and took power by the end of the decade. Beckerman sets beside it the African National Congress after its 1960 ban and its 1969 Morogoro conference, comparing these movements to actors rehearsing in an empty theater for a future they intend to inhabit.

He closes with Octavia Butler's *Parable of the Sower*, in which Lauren Olamina founds Earthseed in a collapsing America. Her teaching is that change is God, and her followers walk north with her. For Beckerman she lives as though on the cusp of something new, which is the presumption the whole essay has followed. The piece is adapted from his book *How to Be a Dissident*.
