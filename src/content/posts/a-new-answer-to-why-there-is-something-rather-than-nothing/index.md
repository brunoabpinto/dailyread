Brenner starts from the old puzzle of why anything exists at all. Leibniz, Schopenhauer and Wittgenstein all found it astonishing. Carl Hempel argued that no explanation can work, because any explanation must cite something that exists, and that thing is part of what needs explaining. Brenner tries an explanation anyway. He challenges the assumption behind the puzzle, which is that a world with nothing in it would be the simplest and easiest possibility.

His key move separates two kinds of truth. Ontological truths are about existing things, such as the Earth orbiting the Sun. Non-ontological truths are not. They include negative existentials ("Mechagodzilla doesn't exist"), facts about what is possible or impossible, logic, arithmetic and morality. These would all hold even if nothing existed. So a world with nothing in it is not empty of truths. It carries a lot of unexplained structure, and it is more complex than it first appears.

Brenner then argues that one existing thing could explain many of these truths at once. That would make a populated world simpler overall, much as Darwin's common ancestor explains many species with one posit. He considers three candidates. The first is a simple God, whose decisions and decrees might ground such truths. The second is a natural initial singularity, which is about as simple as anything could be. The third is human beings, through convention or attitude. He judges the first two simpler than the third, because humans are numerous and complex, though he admits that a full comparison goes beyond the essay.

If this holds, the actual world, full as it is, might be the simplest possible one, and Leibniz's intuition would be backwards. The approach needs reality to favour simplicity, and Brenner defends the idea that simplicity can explain why things are as they are, which would also justify Ockham's Razor as a guide to truth. He concedes that the proposal is highly speculative and does not settle why reality is this way rather than some other way.
