The piece is an interview with Stacy Kim. It starts from envy's bad reputation: Cain killing Abel, and the evil-eye amulets found across many cultures. Kim argues that envy can also hold clues about how to orient our lives if we listen to it. It hurts because we dislike feeling behind, and because religious and cultural traditions have taught us to feel ashamed of wanting what others have. She notes that the deadly sins began as something closer to a physician's checklist, signs of a troubled soul that needed attention, rather than grounds for damnation.

Kim says English gives us poor words for thinking about envy. Korean separates benign envy, which sounds like "I admire you and wish I could be like you" and can open a conversation, from the malicious kind. She also separates envy, which is wanting what someone else has, from jealousy, which involves three parties and the fear of losing a relationship. Malicious envy is aimed at the rival, as with the queen in Snow White. Benign envy is aimed at the thing desired, and that makes it a better driver of self-improvement.

Her method is the acronym SIREN, four questions to ask yourself. Is the target *similar* to you? Envy usually points at people close to us, so a second-chair cellist envies the first chair, not a famous soloist. Is the desire *intangible*? What we want is often the feeling a thing promises, such as security or recognition, and other routes may lead there. Is the comparison *relevant*, meaning in a domain that matters to us? And how strong and persistent is the *negative* emotion, which shows how much attention it deserves. Kim describes how she envied a successful writer for years and avoided that writer's books. When she finally read them, it pushed her toward the kind of work she wanted to do.

Kim warns against suppressing envy. Advice to simply be grateful, or to practise the Buddhist idea of sympathetic joy, fails until you have acknowledged and examined the feeling. She puts envy alongside fear, anger and grief as emotions that carry information: fear signals danger, anger signals injustice, grief signals loss, and envy shows what we want. She also gives an example of envy used collectively, in postwar Korea, where national rivalry helped drive development.

On how to respond when you suspect someone envies you, Kim says you can never really tell, since envy has no characteristic facial expression. Her suggestion is to redirect it toward meaningful work, meaningful relationships and contribution, for instance by asking how you could help or deepen the relationship. Her closing point is that someone who never envies is probably not striving.
