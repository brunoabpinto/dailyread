In January 2018, a 27-year-old Ravenna Koenig arrives in Utqiaġvik, on Alaska's northern coast, on a one-year grant to cover climate change for public radio in Fairbanks. She describes the sea ice that has protected the coast and supported Iñupiat whaling for over a thousand years. That ice now forms later and thinner. She also lays out a bind at the center of the region. Oil revenue pays for most of local government, including the work of adapting to warming, while the state's leaders refuse to limit drilling for the same financial reason.

Running alongside the reporting is her relationship with a friend she calls Max, who lives in Washington, DC. She is at least half in love with him but never says so. After she moves north, he gets in touch less and less, while insisting that nothing has changed between them. Over the months she comes to see that she has been living with an "ambiguous loss," a grief for something that hasn't clearly ended.

The central scene is a spring trip onto the ice by snowmobile with a wildlife biologist. The ice looks solid but is really a patchwork that offshore winds can break apart. At its edge, Koenig feels thrill and panic together, and she reads that moment as a picture of her own life, which depends on luck and on forces she can't control.

Other scenes widen the frame. At a fall whale butchering, community members talk about carrying on through loss, and one whaler says he refuses to worry about future generations. Koenig also draws on Iñupiaq writer Laureli Ivanoff's account of losing a traditional hunt. Back in the lower 48 after the job ends, there are blackberry picking with her mother, a summer of wildfire smoke, and swims in cold water.

The essay never cleanly separates ecological grief from personal heartbreak. It suggests that growing up means piling up absences as much as gains, and it ends by admitting that she has lost very little compared with others, yet she will have to learn the lesson of loss again and again.
