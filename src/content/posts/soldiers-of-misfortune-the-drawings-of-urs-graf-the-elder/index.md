Miya Tokumitsu, a curator at Wesleyan University's Davison Art Collection, looks at Urs Graf the Elder (c. 1485–c. 1528). He was a Swiss goldsmith, printmaker and draftsman, and he also fought as a *Reisläufer*, a Swiss mercenary. He fought in at least four campaigns, including Dijon, Marignano and Bicocca. At home in Basel he was jailed again and again for beating his wife, adultery, slander and assault. He fled the city after an attack that left his victim permanently disabled. Basel let him return because it needed a die-cutter for its mint.

Tokumitsu sets his work against the Italian Wars and the rise of professional armies, which new taxes and credit systems paid for. Swiss pikemen had been famous since the Battle of Nancy (1477), and they competed with German Landsknechte. Society saw mercenaries both as glory-seekers and as a dangerous burden. Graf's contemporary Niklaus Manuel was another artist who served as a mercenary.

The essay reads a series of Graf's drawings and woodcuts closely. They include *Standard-Bearer with His Page*, an *Allegory of the Fortunes of War* in which Fortune stands on a sphere next to an hourglass running out, *Landsknecht, Caught by the Devil*, and his scenes of prostitutes. In these images soldiers come across as vain, foolish, vulnerable and cruel. Women are shown as both exploiting others and deserving sympathy, and sex appears as a deal in which the two sides have very unequal stakes. Drawing on Christiane Andersson and Jess Bailey, Tokumitsu points to symbols of impotence and to fantasies of broken bodies.

Her conclusion is that Graf's work carries an "impotent melancholy," not a celebration of war. She compares him with a modern veteran who keeps being drawn back to the battlefield. She suggests his bleak scenes of sex may show some self-awareness, but she warns that we can't be sure what such a thinly documented artist meant. She ends by setting him against Dürer's ideal Christian knight and pointing out that Dürer himself never tried to live up to that ideal.
